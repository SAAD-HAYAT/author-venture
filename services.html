01 — Ghostwriting

You have the story. We do the typing.

The material was never the problem. You have told this story at dinner tables for fifteen years and it lands every time. The problem is the eleven months of drafting between the story and the book.

So we do that part. We interview you until we can hear your sentence rhythm, your jokes, and the words you would never use. Then we write it, and you read it back and recognise yourself.

How the voice work happens
  1. Six to ten recorded interviewsNinety minutes each, transcribed. We ask the questions a reader will ask, not the ones you have already answered.
  2. A voice sheetYour vocabulary, cadence, and tics, written down as rules — so any editor who touches the book later keeps you sounding like you.
  3. A sample chapterBefore the full commitment. If it does not sound like you, we rewrite it or you walk away.
  4. Chapter by chapterDelivered on a schedule, with two revision rounds built into each one.

02 — Book editing

Four different jobs, routinely sold as one.

Paying for the wrong level is the most common way authors waste money on editing. A proofread will not fix a structural problem, and a developmental edit on a finished manuscript is an expensive way to be told it is fine.

Level 01

Developmental editing

Structure, pacing, plot, character, argument. The pass that asks whether the book works at all, and what to move, cut, or write next. Delivered as an editorial letter plus notes in the manuscript.

When the draft is done but something is off
Level 02

Line editing

Sentence by sentence: rhythm, clarity, voice, dialogue, and the four words doing the work of one. Not grammar — this is the pass that makes prose sound like the best version of you.

When the structure is settled and will not move again
Level 03

Copyediting

Grammar, punctuation, consistency, and fact-checks on names, dates, and timelines. You get a style sheet built for your book, and every change arrives tracked for you to accept or reject.

When the writing is final and layout is next
Level 04

Proofreading

The last look, on the typeset file rather than the manuscript: typos, bad breaks, widows and orphans, wrong running heads, a chapter opening on the wrong side. It catches what layout introduced.

After formatting, immediately before you publish
Not sure which?

Answer one question honestly.

Say the sentence that sounds most like your situation out loud. That is usually the level you need.

  • “I'm not sure the story holds together.”Developmental editing
  • “The story works, but the writing feels flat.”Line editing
  • “It reads well. I just need it correct.”Copyediting
  • “It's designed and I'm about to hit publish.”Proofreading

Still unsure? Send 1,000 words. We will edit them at the level we think your manuscript needs and explain why — including when the honest answer is the cheaper pass.

House standards

What every edit follows

Chicago, unless you say otherwise

Fiction and general non-fiction follow the Chicago Manual of Style. Academic and journalistic projects can use APA, MLA, or AP on request.

A style sheet, always

Every project gets a living document of spellings, character details, and timeline facts. It ships with your manuscript so future editors stay consistent.

Tracked changes, never silent ones

Word with track changes on, plus margin comments explaining the reasoning behind anything substantial. Nothing is altered where you cannot see it.

Typical timeline: 4–8 weeks from final manuscript to live listing

Distribution makes a book orderable. It does not make a bookshop order it.

Wide distribution through Ingram puts your title in the catalogue every bookshop and library buys from, at a trade discount they can accept. That is genuinely valuable and it is not the same as shelf placement, which is bought, earned, or negotiated store by store. Anyone promising you a table at the front of a chain is selling something they cannot deliver.

04 — Children's book publishing

From bedtime story to book on the shelf.

Children's books fail on specifics: the wrong word count for the age, text that stumbles when read aloud at bedtime, art that gets sliced by the trim. We start from the age band and work backwards from print.

Ages 0–3

Board book

Up to 100 words. Thick pages, one clear idea per spread, rhythm doing the work that plot does later.

Ages 3–7

Picture book

500–800 words across 32 pages. Words and pictures tell different halves of the story and never repeat each other.

Ages 5–8

Early reader

1,500–3,000 words. Controlled vocabulary, short sentences, an illustration on nearly every page to carry momentum.

Ages 7–10

Chapter book

5,000–12,000 words. Real chapters, spot illustrations, and a plot a child can follow alone at night.
