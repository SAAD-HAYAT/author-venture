Specimen · editing

One paragraph, before and after.

Editing is the service authors find hardest to judge before they buy, because the evidence disappears into the finished book. So here is the evidence, kept.

Before: 68 words

As it arrived

It was at that particular moment in time that Marisol came to the sudden realisation that the small and rather old wooden fishing boat that had been moored down by the harbour wall for what seemed like a very long time had in fact been completely and utterly abandoned by whoever it was that had once owned it, and that nobody at all was coming back for it.

After: 24 words

After a line edit

Marisol realised the old fishing boat by the harbour wall had been abandoned. Nobody was coming back for it.

Same meaning. Same voice. Forty-four words lighter, and now it moves.

Nothing was added that the author did not write. A line edit removes what the sentence is carrying for no reason. It does not rewrite you into someone else. If it reads like a different author afterwards, the edit was wrong.

Specimen · interior

What a typeset page is actually doing.

Readers never notice a good interior, which is exactly the point. These are the decisions behind that invisibility.

Measure

Sixty-odd characters a line

Too wide and the eye loses its place returning to the left margin. Too narrow and it snags every few words. The measure sets the trim size, not the other way round.

Gutter

Room for the spine to take

Inner margins grow with page count. A 400-page paperback needs noticeably more gutter than a 180-page one, or the reader is fighting the binding to read the first word of every line.

Widows & orphans

No stranded lines

A single line marooned at the top of a page is the clearest tell of an unedited layout. Fixing them is slow, manual, and the difference between a book and a printed document.

Chapter openers

A consistent drop

Every chapter starts at the same depth, with the same relationship between number, title, and first line. Consistency is invisible; inconsistency is all a reader sees.

Worked example

What a finished project looks like end to end.

A composite of how a full memoir engagement runs, written out so you can see where the time and the money actually go. Not a client account, but a map of the process.

A memoir arrives as 140,000 words of notes, half-chapters, and voice memos recorded in a car. Developmental editing cuts it to 82,000 and reorders the whole thing around three turning points that were buried in the middle. A line edit follows, then a copyedit, then design, layout, and a proof. Fourteen weeks after the first call it is live on Amazon and orderable through Ingram by any bookshop in the country.

The author keeps every right, sets the price herself, and takes the full royalty on every copy. That is the shape of the engagement, whoever the author turns out to be.

Range

Genres we work in.

Different genres fail in different ways. A thriller dies on pacing, a memoir on structure, a business book on the second half. Knowing which trap yours is walking toward is most of the job.

Memoir & life story

The order is never the order it happened in

Chronology is rarely the best structure for a life. Most memoirs improve enormously the moment they stop starting at birth.

Business & leadership

One idea, properly proven

The common failure is six ideas at 60% depth. We push for the single argument you can defend, and the case studies that carry it.

Fiction

Pacing is structural, not stylistic

When readers stop at chapter nine it is almost never the prose. It is a scene that has no consequence, sitting where a turn should be.

Children’s

Read it aloud or you will not hear the problem

Every children’s manuscript we edit gets read out at full length. Stumbles on the page become obvious the second they are spoken.
